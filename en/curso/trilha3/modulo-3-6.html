🦾 Humanoids in Production

Humanoid robots from Figure, Boston Dynamics e UBTECH are moving from the pilot phase to high-volume production at factories like BMW and BYD. That’s the leap that completely changes the training dynamic: humanoids operate in human spaces, requiring new skills for coexistence.

🤖 Why humanoids are different

A robotic arm stays in an isolated cell. A humanoid walks across the factory floor, sharing aisles and workbenches with workers. Training shifts from “how to operate the machine” to “how to coexist with an artificial colleague.”

  • •Figure 02 — general-purpose humanoid in automotive production tests.
  • •Boston Dynamics — Electric Atlas moving from demo to industrial task.
  • •UBTECH Walker S2 — assembly-line deployment in China.

📊 Market Signals

  • BMW and BYD — first factories to receive humanoids outside the pilot phase.
  • Co-workspace training — a new training category: humans and humanoids in the same area.
  • Volume — the “pilot to production” transition is the trigger that creates demand for reskilling at scale.

💡 Practical Tip

Don't wait for the humanoid to arrive before creating the training. Whoever prepares coexistence competencies before of deployment becomes a preferred supplier when the factory needs to upskill its team in a hurry.

📏 Stricter HRC Regulation

Countries are creating national equivalents of ISO 10218:2025, with additional local requirements. Human-robot collaboration (HRC) regulations are becoming stricter and more fragmented by region. For those operating in multiple countries, this means training adapted to each jurisdiction—and complexity creates demand for specialists.

✓ How to position yourself

  • ✓Map local requirements by jurisdiction where the client operates.
  • ✓Offer modular training adaptable by country.
  • ✓Treat multi-jurisdictional compliance as a premium differentiator.
  • ✓Maintain the foundation in ISO 10218 and add national layers.

✗ What to avoid

  • ✗Assume that a single training program works for every country.
  • ✗Ignore additional local requirements until the end of the rollout.
  • ✗Treating fragmentation as a problem instead of an opportunity.
  • ✗Selling compliance as a low-value commodity.

💡 Practical Tip

Regulatory fragmentation may seem like an obstacle, but it is precisely what creates value for a local specialist. Where the rules are simple and global, anyone can provide training. Where they are complex and regional, you can charge a premium.

🥽 VR/AR as the Standard

VR/AR is becoming the onboarding standard for robotic environments. The argument is twofold: 38% reduction in training time plus risk elimination during learning. Anyone offering training without VR/AR will be seen as obsolete — the technology is no longer a differentiator; it becomes the baseline.

📊 Research Data

  • 38% faster — reduction in training time with VR onboarding.
  • Zero risk — learning without exposure to the physical robot during the initial learning curve.
  • Digital twin — virtual replica of the real environment for safe practice.

🎯 From differentiator to baseline

Every technology follows the same curve: it starts as a competitive advantage and ends as a minimum expectation. VR/AR in robotics training has passed that point. In 2027-2030, offering training without VR/AR will be like selling training "with only a paper manual."

  • •AR-assisted — instructions overlaid in the operator’s field of view.
  • •VR onboarding — first exposure to the environment without physical risk.
  • •Risk-free training — an error in the virtual environment won’t injure the worker or damage the machine.

🌐 120M at risk

The World Economic Forum (WEF) estimates 120 million global workers at risk of redundancy through automation. Of these, 11 out of every 100 will be impossible to reskill because of the pace of change. The retraining window is closing: anyone who doesn’t start now won’t have time to train the required number by 2030.

⚠️ Time urgency

Large-scale reskilling takes years: create content, train trainers, run cohorts, measure results. If redundancy arrives before training, the 11% impossible to reskill stop being statistics and become people with no path forward. The window is narrow by design.

1

Now — window of opportunity open

2026-2027

Emerging demand, few mature suppliers. Those who start now build volume and establish a position ahead of the competition.

2

Peak — window closing

2028-2029

Redundancy accelerates with humanoids in production. Demand explodes, but training trainers takes time—the bottleneck isn't money, it's teaching capacity.

3

Limit — 11% with no path forward

2030

For 11 out of every 100, the pace of change has made reskilling unfeasible. The social cost defines the scale of responsibility—and opportunity—for those working in this market.

💡 The Central Thesis

The thesis underpinning the entire course: "equip people to work alongside, supervise, and make decisions in automated systems". It’s not about operating robots—it’s about coexisting with them. The future isn’t "human vs. robot," but "human with robot." The core competency is knowing how to work alongside, supervise, and make decisions within the hybrid system.

🧭 Coexist, supervise, decide

Three verbs define human competence in the hybrid system. They replace the old “operate”—which assumes the machine is a passive tool. In an automated system, the machine acts; the human coexists, supervises, and decides.

  • •Coexist — sharing space and pace with the robot safely and confidently.
  • •Supervise — monitor the system, read signals, intervene at the right moment.
  • •Decide — take on the judgment automation cannot provide: context, ethics, exceptions.

✓ Human with robot

  • ✓A hybrid system where each part does what it does best.
  • ✓Humans contribute judgment, context, and decision-making.
  • ✓Coexistence (human-robot coexistence) as a trainable competency.

✗ Human vs. robot

  • ✗Frame it as a dispute over who gets to keep their job.
  • ✗Reduce the human to a button pusher for the machine.
  • ✗Train isolated tasks and ignore coexistence within the system.

💡 Practical Tip

Whenever you describe training, replace the verb “operate” with “work alongside, supervise, and make decisions.” This change in language immediately communicates that you're selling a transformation in capabilities, not operating instructions.

🎯 Positioning

The positioning that brings the course to a close: being a human capacity transformation company, not operational training. The difference lies in depth, not the task. Operational training is a commodity; human capability transformation is premium. The market needs the latter but only finds the former—that’s the gap.

✓ Transformation (premium)

  • ✓Develops the ability to coexist, supervise, and make decisions.
  • ✓Measures behavior change, not class hours.
  • ✓Depth comes from an ongoing relationship with the client.
  • ✓Hard to copy, high perceived value.

✗ Operational (commodity)

  • ✗Teaches only how to perform the task at hand.
  • ✗Sold by the hour or per person, with pricing pressure.
  • ✗Interchangeable — any supplier delivers the same thing.
  • ✗Occasional relationship, without differentiation.

📊 The market gap

  • Demand — the market needs to transform human capabilities.
  • Offering — mostly finds operational training (a commodity).
  • Gap — whoever closes this gap claims the premium position with almost no competition.

🚀 The strategic move

Bring together the projections: humanoids in production, fragmented regulation, VR/AR as the baseline, 120M at risk, and the window closing. Add the central thesis. The result is a single defensible position: transforming human capabilities to coexist with robots — premium, urgent, and difficult to copy.
