MODULE 1.3

📋 ISO 10218:2025 — The New Era of HRC Safety

The first revision since 2011 rewrites the foundation of industrial robotics standards. The concept of a “collaborative robot” as a category disappears, TS 15066 is absorbed, cybersecurity becomes a physical safety requirement, and the Class I / Class II classification is introduced. Understanding this standard is the dividing line between installing safely and facing legal action.

📅 First Revision Since 2011

A ISO 10218:2025 replaces the 2011 version and is the first revision in 14 years. During that time, industrial robotics changed in nature: cobots, AMRs, embedded AI, and cyberattacks on the factory floor arrived. Published in Q1 2025, the standard incorporates all of this and redefines what it means to "work alongside a robot."

2011

ISO 10218:2011 — Parts 1 and 2

The era of the caged robot

An industrial robot is a dangerous machine that operates behind fences. Human-robot collaboration was the exception, not the rule. Cobots were still in the lab.

2016

ISO/TS 15066 — Technical Specification

Force and pressure limits

A separate document that defined biomechanical force and pressure limits for collaboration. It was the reference, but it did not have full standards status.

2025

ISO 10218:2025 — The consolidation

The era of collaborative operations

Incorporates TS 15066, eliminates "cobot" as a category, integrates cybersecurity, and creates Class I / Class II. A 3-year transition period to revalidate existing installations.

📊 Why this matters now

  • 14 years - time between the two revisions of the standard
  • 3 years - transition period to revalidate installations
  • Q1 2025 - official publication of the new version
  • Shortage - robotic safety professionals are rare and in high demand

2

🚫 “Collaborative robot” eliminated as a category

The standard's most profound change is conceptual: there is no longer such a thing as a "collaborative robot" as a type of robot. Any robot can operate collaboratively, as long as the application meet the safety requirements. Risk analysis shifts its focus from the machine to its actual use.

🎯 The paradigm shift

The question has changed. Before: “Is this robot collaborative?” Now: “Does this application operate safely and collaboratively?”

  • •The focus shifts away from the equipment and toward task, layout, and interaction.
  • •A “heavy” robot can run in collaborative mode if the application limits speed and force.
  • •A “lightweight” cobot can be dangerous if the tool at its tip can cut or puncture.

✗ Old logic (2011)

  • ✗"Collaborative robot" as a product category
  • ✗Risk assessment focused on the machine
  • ✗Buying the cobot already took care of "safety"
  • ✗Marketing sold “safe robot by design”

✓ New logic (2025)

  • ✓"Collaborative operation" as a mode of use
  • ✓Risk assessment for the complete application
  • ✓Safety depends on the robot + tool + task + environment
  • ✓Shared integrator/employer responsibility

💡 Practical Tip

When a vendor says “this is a collaborative robot, so it’s already safe,” correct them mentally: no robot is safe on its own. What is (or isn’t) safe is the application. Always ask for the cell’s risk assessment, not the arm’s datasheet.

📐 ISO/TS 15066 absorbed — PFL and SSM integrated

O Technical Specification 15066 — which defined force and pressure limits for collaboration — ceased to be a separate document and was absorbed by ISO 10218:2025. As a result, the four collaborative modes gain full regulatory requirements, more rigorous and integrated. PFL and SSM, in particular, now have verifiable and auditable limits.

1 · SMS — Safety-rated Monitored Stop

The robot stops when a human enters the collaborative zone and resumes only when they leave. A safety-rated stop, not a power shutdown.

2 · HG — Hand Guiding

The operator physically guides the robot, hand over hand, using a guiding device with a safety function. Used for teach-in and assisted movement.

3 · SSM — Speed and Separation Monitoring

Sensors maintain a minimum safe distance; the robot slows down as the human approaches and stops if the separation falls below the limit.

4 · PFL — Power and Force Limiting

Human contact is permitted, but force and pressure stay below the biomechanical limits of the former TS 15066. This mode depends most on the robot hardware.

📏 TS 15066 (PFL) legacy

Biomechanical limits by body region remain a normative reference within ISO 10218:2025:

body_region    pressure_limit   force_limit
face / skull   not allowed    not allowed
hands / fingers     more restrictive  low
torso / abdomen intermediate   intermediate
legs / thighs   more permissive  higher

Illustrative table of the PFL logic — exact values by region are specified in the standard. Each region has its own pressure/force pair.

💡 Practical Tip

A cell can combine modes over time: SSM while the robot works alone, transitioning to PFL or SMS when a human enters. Documenting this transition in the risk assessment is where most audits find gaps.

🔒 Cybersecurity = Physical Safety

For the first time, cybersecurity becomes a physical safety requirement into a robotics standard. The logic is straightforward: an attack that changes the force parameters of a cobot, or disables a separation sensor, is no longer an “IT problem” and becomes an risk of injury. Safety and security converge.

🚨 Compliance and risk alert

A network-connected cobot with remotely accessible force parameters is, under ISO 10218:2025, a physical security vulnerability. If an attacker raises the force limits, the robot stops being PFL-safe without any operator noticing.

  • ⚠Safety-rated parameters should never be writable without strong authentication.
  • ⚠The cell's OT network must be segmented from the corporate (IT) network.
  • ⚠Parameter change logs become legal evidence in the event of an accident.

🔗 IT / OT convergence

Two worlds that used to be separate now intersect within the same robotic cell:

  • •IT (Information Technology): networks, authentication, encryption, patch management.
  • •OT (Operational Technology): PLCs, robot controllers, safety sensors.
  • •Defense-in-depth: multiple layers — network segmentation, access control, monitoring.
  • •IEC 62443: industrial cybersecurity standard used as a complementary reference.

🧩 What Changes for the Professional

  • New competency - robotic safety professionals now need to understand cyber.
  • IEC 62443 - complementary reference for the industrial security layer.
  • Attacks on safety-rated parameters - they become documented physical risk vectors.

⚙️ Class I vs Class II

The standard establishes a new classification for robotic systems. Class I are systems with safety functions inherent (force sensors, built-in speed limits). Class II are systems that depend on external protective devices to be safe. Getting the class wrong means applying the wrong requirements — and being held responsible for it.

Class I — Built-in safety

  • ✓Safety functions inherent to the system
  • ✓Integrated force sensors and speed limits
  • ✓Less reliance on external protection
  • ✓Risk assessment focused on the system configuration

Class II — Add-on safety

  • •Depends on external protective devices
  • •Fences, area scanners, light curtains
  • •Greater integration complexity and cost
  • •Risk assessment focused on additional devices

💡 Practical Tip

The class changes the cost. A Class II system requires fences, scanners, and light curtains — all of which weigh on the integration budget. Classifying too low exposes the worker; classifying too high adds unnecessary cost. The correct class comes from the risk assessment, not intuition.

⚖️ Legal risk

Misclassifying can mean inadequate compliance. In the event of an accident, the classification recorded in the safety project is the first document that experts and authorities examine. A Class II cell treated as Class I is documented negligence.
