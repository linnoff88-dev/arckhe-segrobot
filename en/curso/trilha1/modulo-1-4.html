🧠 Psychology of Resistance: Why Humans Reject Robots

Amygdala, technostress, maladaptive behaviors, and the fear-incompetence-resistance cycle. Resistance to robots is not stubbornness—it is biology. Those who understand the mechanism can calibrate confidence, deactivate the alarm, and turn rejection into adoption.

1

🫀 Amygdala and fight-or-flight

A amygdala processes threats in milliseconds — even before the rational cortex comes into play. When the brain realizes that a robot could “replace” the worker, it triggers the same response as fight-or-flight of a physical threat. This is not drama: it is survival biology operating in a context it was not designed to interpret.

⚡ Amygdala Hijack: Emotional Hijacking

When the alarm goes off, cortisol floods the body and cognitive ability plummets. The worker is in “emotional flooding”—unable to absorb technical content. Presenting an operation manual in that state is like throwing information at a closed door.

  • •Amygdala hijack — emotion hijacks reason in fractions of a second.
  • •Cortisol reduces cognitive capacity and working memory.
  • •Emotional flooding in an organizational context blocks learning.

💡 Practical Tip

Deactivate the alarm before to present the technical content. Start every session with psychological safety ("no one will be fired for using the wrong robot in the first few weeks") and only then move on to the technical part. The order matters more than the content.

😰 Technostress and Psychological Contract

Technostress is the stress caused by an inability to cope with new technologies. The introduction of robots violates the psychological contract implicit from the worker: "I did my job right, so my job is safe." When this invisible agreement breaks, trust in the organization collapses along with it.

📊 The 5 creators of technostress

  • 1.Techno-overload — the robot requires working faster and for longer.
  • 2.Techno-invasion — technology crosses boundaries of time and space.
  • 3.Techno-complexity — complexity makes workers feel inadequate.
  • 4.Techno-insecurity — fear of being replaced by someone who masters the technology (or by the machine itself).
  • 5.Techno-uncertainty — constant changes make it impossible to master the system.

⚠️ The hidden cost of ignoring it

Technostress causes absenteeism (lack), presenteeism (body present, mind absent) and turnover (output). Ignoring this factor costs more than the investment in the robot itself — because an expensive robot operated by a broken team never achieves the projected ROI.

3

🔥 Maladaptive Behaviors (APA)

A APA documents three patterns of maladaptive responses to automation: sabotage (damage equipment), avoidance (refuse interaction) and somatization (pain and insomnia attributed to the robot). Recognizing them as symptoms — not vandalism or laziness — is what separates a supervisor who addresses the root cause from one who only punishes.

✓ How an attentive supervisor responds

  • ✓Read sabotage as distress call, not as a crime.
  • ✓Investigates the root cause of the behavior.
  • ✓Intervenes early, before the pattern becomes entrenched.
  • ✓Refer cases of somatization for occupational health support.

✗ What NOT to do

  • ✗Treating sabotage as simple "vandalism."
  • ✗Punish avoidance without understanding the fear behind it.
  • ✗Dismiss physical complaints as “being fussy.”
  • ✗Wait for the standard to become part of the culture before responding.

🔬 Nuances of the three standards

  • •Passive vs. active sabotage — from “forgetting” to turn on the robot to deliberate damage.
  • •Selective avoidance — uses the robot only when being watched.
  • •Somatization — correlates with physical proximity to the robot.

4

🔁 Fear-Incompetence-Resistance Cycle

Fear of the robot leads to avoidance; avoidance prevents learning; lack of learning leads to incompetence; incompetence feeds more fear. It is a vicious cycle that reinforces itself — and can only be broken with deliberate intervention. The key is to tackle the most accessible link: almost always, reducing fear before demanding competence.

1

Psychological safety first

Cycle entry point

Before any technical training, make sure mistakes aren’t punished. Without that, none of the following steps will stick.

2

Gradual exposure (proximity ladder)

Gradual desensitization

Bring workers closer to the robot in stages: observe, stand nearby, touch the panel, operate under supervision, operate independently. Each stage reduces fear of the next.

3

Competency quick wins

Breaks the incompetence link

Deliver quick, visible wins: successfully completing the first task builds a sense of competence, which in turn reduces fear. The loop starts working in your favor.

4

Peer social proof

Consolidates the change

Testimonials from coworkers — not management — are the most powerful form of social proof. “If João can do it, I can too” overcomes resistance better than any slide.

💡 Practical Tip

Don't try to break all four links at the same time. Choose an entry point — almost always psychological safety — and attack only it. When the cycle stops turning at one point, it loses strength everywhere else.

Key concepts

Vicious cycle

Self-reinforcing.

Proximity ladder

Gradual exposure.

Quick wins

Sense of competence.

Social proof

5

🛡️ Physical + psychological safety (Brookings)

O Brookings Institution argues that safety when working with robots has two dimensions: physical (the robot must not cause harm) and psychological (the worker must not feel they will be replaced). Standards such as ISO 10218 cover the first. There is no standard for the second — and this is exactly the gap the reskilling professional fills.

🧩 Dual safety framework

The two types of safety are interdependent: a physically safe but psychologically threatening environment still creates resistance; and psychological safety without physical safety is irresponsible. They need to go hand in hand.

  • •Physical safety — covered by ISO 10218; barriers, sensors, emergency stop.
  • •Psychological safety — no standard; a prerequisite for learning (Edmondson).
  • •Indicators — turnover, absenteeism, and internal NPS measure the psychological dimension.

✓ Dual-safety environment

  • ✓The team can admit mistakes without fear of retaliation.
  • ✓ISO 10218 implemented and visible on the factory floor.
  • ✓Workplace climate indicators tracked (turnover, internal NPS).
  • ✓Clear message: “the robot enhances you; it doesn’t replace you.”

✗ False sense of security

  • ✗Follow only the physical safety standard and assume that’s enough.
  • ✗Punish those who report near misses or questions.
  • ✗Treat turnover and absenteeism as noise.
  • ✗Leave the fear of layoffs hanging in the air, unanswered.

⚖️ Trust Calibration

Over-trust and trust the robot too much: ignore alarms, skip checks. Under-trust and not trust enough: check everything manually, override the robot's decisions. Both are dangerous. The ideal level of trust is calibrated — the human knows when the robot is reliable and when to intervene.

✓ Calibrated confidence

  • ✓Knows how to distinguish tasks the robot can perform reliably from those that require supervision.
  • ✓Uses system transparency to decide when to intervene.
  • ✓Adjusts trust through continuous feedback loops.
  • ✓Treat the robot as a tool, not as an oracle or an enemy.

✗ Miscalibrated confidence

  • ✗Over-trust: ignores alarms and skips checks (automation complacency).
  • ✗Over-trust: accepts the robot's decision even when it contradicts the evidence (automation bias).
  • ✗Under-trust: manually rechecks everything, nullifying the gain.
  • ✗Under-trust: overrides the robot's correct decisions out of distrust.

💡 Practical Tip

Transparency and the main calibration tool. When the robot shows why it makes each decision, the operator learns to predict when it gets things right and when it gets them wrong. Training without transparency inevitably produces one of two extremes.
