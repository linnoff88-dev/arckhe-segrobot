This module covers what changes on the hospital floor and the HARMONY project's multi-robot coordination.

📊 Market: USD 16B to 45+B

The healthcare robotics market grows from USD 16 billion for more than 45 billion by 2030, driven by a shortage of professionals and demand for efficiency. For healthcare professionals, this means that working alongside robots is no longer hypothetical — it's become a job requirement.

🧭 Core concept: four segments, one common ground

Growth isn’t uniform. It spans segments with distinct dynamics, but they all reach the same hospital floor:

  • •Surgical — assistance with procedures, high precision.
  • •Logistics — Medication, instruments, and waste AMRs (this module's focus).
  • •Rehabilitation — exoskeletons and assisted therapy.
  • •Disinfection — UV-C robots and environmental decontamination.

📈 Market data

  • USD 16bn → 45+bn - healthcare robot market size by 2030.
  • ~3x - the market nearly triples in 5 years.
  • Drivers - Professional shortages, pressure to improve efficiency, aging population.
  • Barrier - Cost of adoption and integration with existing infrastructure.

💡 Practical tip

Professionals who understand hospital robots have a competitive advantage in a market that will triple in 5 years. Keep up with regulations FDA / ANVISA: it defines which robots can operate and under what conditions—knowing the regulatory framework makes you a go-to person on the team.

🤖 Hospital AMRs

Autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) transport medications, sterile instruments, and hospital waste, operating 24/7 in corridors shared with staff and patients. Nurses spend up to 30% of the shift on logistics tasks—and the AMR frees up that time for direct care.

⚙️ What a Hospital AMR Needs to Master

  • •Elevator integration — call, get in, and ride between floors on its own.
  • •Sanitation Protocol — sterile cargo must not be contaminated in transit.
  • •Cargo traceability — who sent it, what it carries, who received it.
  • •Navigation in a dynamic environment — crowded corridors, stretchers, visitors.

✓ What the AMR solves

  • ✓Repetitive transport of medication and instruments.
  • ✓Collecting waste on a fixed route without exposing the team to risk.
  • ✓24/7 operation without fatigue, including overnight.
  • ✓Gives nurses up to 30% of their shift back for patient care.

✗ What the AMR does NOT do

  • ✗Clinical decision or patient triage.
  • ✗Replace the judgment about load urgency.
  • ✗Cutting the line on your own during an emergency.
  • ✗Operate without a defined sanitation protocol.

👩‍⚕️ 78% of Nurses Approve

Research indicates that 78% of nurses that work with AMRs report improvements in their daily workflow, with reduced physical fatigue and more time with patients. But acceptance is not automatic — the 22% dissatisfied point to real problems.

📊 Satisfaction data

  • 78% - report improvements in their daily workflow.
  • 22% - dissatisfied, with concrete, actionable complaints.
  • Benefits - less physical fatigue, more time for direct care.
  • Metric - Internal NPS is how you measure adoption over time.

✓ Satisfaction factors

  • ✓Less physical strain from transportation.
  • ✓More time with the patient.
  • ✓Adequate training during onboarding.
  • ✓Clear and predictable interface.

✗ Rejection factors (the 22%)

  • ✗Confusing call interface.
  • ✗Lack of training.
  • ✗A robot that “gets in the way” during emergencies.
  • ✗Without a feedback loop for reporting problems.

💡 Practical tip

The adoption curve varies by profile: early adopters embrace the robot early, resistant need proof. Anyone who understands both sides — and takes the 22% seriously — becomes a bridge between the team and management during implementation.

📚 What to Learn

The practical skills hospital teams need: understanding the navigation of the robot, such as call and cancel deliveries, and—crucially—when DO NOT trust in the system. Most problems with hospital robots aren't technical: they're about use.

🎓 The competency pathway

Poorly trained professionals underuse the system (leave the robot idle and do everything by hand) or overuse it (send everything to the robot, including urgent loads that cannot wait in a queue). The key is to master the full cycle: call → prioritize → bypass when needed → escalate.

1

Call interface

Basic operation

Knowing how to call and cancel a delivery in the interface — the entry point for all AMR use.

2

Delivery prioritization

Judgment

Decide what goes to the robot and what is handled manually, based on the urgency of the load.

3

Manual bypass

When NOT to trust

Urgent loads that cannot wait for the robot's queue should be carried by hand — recognizing this case is a skill, not a failure.

4

Maintenance and escalation

First level

Resolve small problems (first-level maintenance) and escalate to technical support when the case is beyond your reach.

🧬 Robot Adjusts Trajectory Based on Urgency

Research published in Science Robotics (2023) describes robots that adjust their route and speed based on urgency signals in the hospital environment — alarms, staff movement. Robots that "understand" urgency reduce conflicts with staff.

🧠 Context-aware navigation

The practical difference is enormous: an AMR that stops in the hallway during a blue code is a problem. One that yields and waits is an ally. Research shows that reading the context changes the robot from an obstacle into a partner.

  • •Social cues — the robot reads the team's social cues as they work.
  • •Dynamic prioritization — recalculates the route as urgency changes.
  • •HIS integration — connects to the hospital information system.

⚠️ Attention: the blue code case

A robot that can’t read urgency can literally block the corridor while the team rushes to respond. Context-aware navigation isn’t a luxury—it’s patient safety. Evaluate this criterion when assessing any hospital AMR.

💡 Practical tip

The system learns urgency patterns through machine learning. The more the team reports situations (ignored alarm, wrong detour), the better the robot gets. Human feedback is the fuel for intelligent navigation.

🔗 EU HARMONY and Critical Failure

The European project HARMONY (EU H2020) coordinates multiple robots in a hospital environment — and defines the response protocol when a critical delivery (blood, organs) fails in transit. Hospital multi-robot systems require orchestration.

🚦 The corridor priority problem

If two AMRs cross paths in the aisle, both carrying critical loads, who has priority? The HARMONY protocol defines that before to happen — hospital fleet management and load priority are decided in the project, not improvised.

1

Failure detected in transit

Critical delivery (blood/organ)

The system detects that the AMR is stuck or has lost its route with critical cargo on board.

2

Manual fallback activated

Human takes over

The team is alerted and takes over the task manually—the robot is never the sole point of failure for a critical load.

3

Chain of custody preserved

Traceability

The cargo chain of custody (who had possession, and when) is maintained from the robot to the human, with no break in traceability.

💡 Practical tip

The professional who knows the critical handoff protocol fails is who keeps the hospital running when the robot stalls. Knowing how to activate the manual fallback and preserve the chain of custody is a safety skill, not a technical detail.
